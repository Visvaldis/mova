Take the Latin word *pater* and the English word *father*. Or *piscis* and *fish*. Or *pēs* and *foot*. Notice the pattern? Where Latin has **p**, English has **f**. Every time.

This is not a quirk. It is one clause of a sweeping rule discovered in 1822 by Jacob Grimm — yes, the fairy-tale Grimm — and it changed linguistics from stamp collecting into a science.

## Grimm's Law

Sometime in the first millennium BCE, the ancestors of the Germanic languages ran their entire stop-consonant system through a chain shift:

The breathy stops *bʰ, dʰ, gʰ* hardened into *b, d, g*. The plain voiced *b, d, g* devoiced into *p, t, k*. And the plain voiceless *p, t, k* softened into the fricatives *f, θ, h*. Three dominoes, one rotation.

That single rotation explains why Latin *trēs* is English *three* (t → θ), why *cornu* is *horn* (k → h), why *dent-* is *tooth* (d → t), and why Ukrainian *два* — which kept the older Slavic consonant — lines up with English *two* (d → t). Ukrainian and Latin often preserve what English transformed, which is why comparing them is like seeing before-and-after photos of the same word.

> Sound change is the most law-like phenomenon in all of human culture: when a shift happens, it happens to every eligible word in the language.

## The Neogrammarian bet

In the 1870s a group of young German linguists made a bold claim: sound laws admit **no exceptions**. Apparent exceptions just mean you haven't found the second law yet. The claim held up spectacularly — the messy exceptions to Grimm's Law were resolved by Verner's Law (1875), which showed the outcome depended on where the ancient accent fell. Regularity all the way down.

This is what makes reconstruction possible. If change were random, the past would be unrecoverable. Because it is regular, linguists can run the rules backwards, like rewinding a film, from *father* and *pater* to PIE \*ph₂tḗr.

## Shifts are still happening

Sound change never stopped. The Great Vowel Shift (English, 1400–1700) rotated every long vowel — it's why English spelling looks deranged: *bite, meet, house* are spelled for their medieval pronunciations. Ukrainian had its own signature shift: the change of *o* and *e* to *і* in closed syllables (*кінь* from *конь*, *ніч* from *ночь*) — one of the features that makes Ukrainian instantly recognizable among Slavic languages. And right now, in the 21st century, you can watch shifts mid-flight: the cot–caught merger spreading through North America, or th-fronting (*think* → *fink*) advancing through British English.

Nobody decides these changes. Nobody can stop them. Each generation slightly re-hears the language of its parents, and over a thousand years the drift becomes a new language.
