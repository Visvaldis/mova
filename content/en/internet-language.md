For most of history, writing was formal by default — slow, edited, addressed to posterity. Then, within one generation, billions of people started writing constantly, instantly, and informally. Linguist Gretchen McCulloch calls this the rise of "informal writing," and it is arguably the biggest natural experiment in the history of language.

## Emoji are gestures, not words

The most common fear — emoji are replacing words — gets the linguistics backwards. McCulloch's analysis shows emoji behave like *gesture*: they do in writing what hands, eyebrows, and tone do in speech. A 😉 doesn't replace a sentence; it tells you how to take one. That's why nobody writes novels in emoji, and why dropping emoji from a friendly message can read as cold: it's the textual equivalent of speaking in a flat voice with your hands tied.

The same logic explains the "typographical tone of voice": the difference between *ok*, *ok.*, *OK*, *okay!!*, and *kk* is not spelling chaos — it's a precise, evolving system for encoding attitude that every fluent internet user reads instantly.

> We didn't lose tone of voice when we moved online. We rebuilt it out of punctuation, capitals, and 💀.

## Change at internet speed

Sound shifts used to take centuries. Internet lexical change takes months. Words rise (*rizz*, *delulu*), peak, and become "cringe" markers of last year within a single school cycle. The mechanism is classical — innovation, diffusion through social networks, prestige dynamics — but the network is now global and the cycle compressed a hundredfold. Linguists can finally watch language change in high resolution, with timestamps.

Ukrainian internet language runs the same experiment in parallel: English borrowings adapt to Cyrillic and Ukrainian morphology (*кринж*, *рофлити*, *скролити*, *забанити*), native coinages compete with them, and wartime reality generates vocabulary at record speed — *бавовна*, *приліт*, *двіж* — much of it studied by Ukrainian linguists in real time.

## And now: AI enters the chat

Large language models add a strange new force. For the first time, a huge share of new text is produced by non-humans trained on yesterday's human text. Early effects are already visible: words statistically favored by chatbots (*delve*, *tapestry*) measurably rose in human writing, and kids use "that's so AI" as an insult meaning *fake*. Whether AI will homogenize style, accelerate change, or freeze it by endlessly recycling the past is one of the most interesting open questions in linguistics — and the subject of our next article.
