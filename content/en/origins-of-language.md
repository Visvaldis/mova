Every human community on Earth — without a single exception — has a fully expressive language. No animal does. Somewhere between our split from the chimpanzee lineage roughly seven million years ago and the first cave paintings, something happened that has no parallel in the history of life.

The puzzle is that language leaves no fossils. Sound vanishes; brains rot. So researchers triangulate from everything else: genes, skulls, archaeology, the babbling of infants, and the structure of the 7,000 languages alive today.

## The new timeline: 135,000 years

For most of the 20th century, the question "when did language emerge?" was considered so unanswerable that the Linguistic Society of Paris famously banned debate on it in 1866. That changed with genomics.

A 2025 study led by MIT linguist Shigeru Miyagawa took a clever shortcut: every human population alive today has language, so language capacity must predate the earliest population split in our species. Genomic data places that first split around 135,000 years ago. Language — at least the *capacity* for it — must be at least that old. Archaeological traces of symbolic behavior (ochre engraving, shell beads, burial goods) cluster around 100,000 years ago, suggesting a gap: we may have carried language inwardly, as a tool of thought, before it reshaped culture.

> Language may have begun as a private system within thought — and only later become the engine of human culture.

## Gesture first, or voice first?

How it started is still contested. Three families of hypotheses dominate:

**Gesture-first.** Apes gesture flexibly but vocalize rigidly. Sign languages are full languages. Perhaps language began in the hands and migrated to the mouth, freeing the hands for tools.

**Vocal-first.** Human speech anatomy — a lowered larynx, fine breath control, the FOXP2 gene variant tied to articulation — looks heavily selected. Why pay that evolutionary cost if voice came late?

**Gradualism.** No single leap. Protolanguage — words without much grammar, like the speech of a toddler — may have existed for hundreds of thousands of years, with full syntax arriving late and possibly fast.

## Why only us?

Vervet monkeys have alarm calls; bees dance directions to nectar; whales sing dialects. But all animal systems are closed — a fixed list of signals. Human language is *combinatorial*: a finite set of sounds builds infinite sentences, including ones never uttered before (like this one). The leap to combinatorial structure, more than any single sound or sign, is the thing that needs explaining — and the thing that, so far, only happened once.

## What to watch

The field is moving fast: ancient DNA is recovering speech-related gene variants from Neanderthal genomes, and computational models now test how compositional structure can self-organize among communicating agents. The Paris ban is well and truly over.
