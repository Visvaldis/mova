Most invented languages serve a story. Tolkien ran the arrow backwards. "The invention of languages is the foundation," he wrote to his publisher. "The 'stories' were made rather to provide a world for the languages than the reverse." *The Lord of the Rings*, by its author's own account, is the longest piece of worldbuilding ever committed for the sake of grammar.

He came by it honestly. Tolkien was a philologist — a professional historical linguist — professor of Anglo-Saxon at Oxford, and before that a junior editor on the *Oxford English Dictionary*, tracing etymologies for words near the beginning of W. He had been inventing languages since childhood, through schoolboy codes into ever more serious designs, and in 1931 confessed the habit in a lecture titled *A Secret Vice*: language invention as a private art form, as legitimate as music and as embarrassing to admit.

## Two flavors of Elvish

The two great Elvish languages are aesthetic opposites built from real-world loves. **Quenya**, the ancient ceremonial "Elf-Latin", is modeled on the sound of Finnish, which Tolkien discovered through the *Kalevala* and described as intoxicating — long vowels, soft consonants, words like *Namárië* and *elen síla lúmenn' omentielvo*, "a star shines on the hour of our meeting." **Sindarin**, the living vernacular of Middle-earth's elves, is built on the music of Welsh — its mutating consonants and falling rhythms, the language of *Mithrandir* and *A Elbereth Gilthoniel*.

The choice of models was itself a linguistic statement: Tolkien believed languages had distinct tastes, that sound itself carried beauty before meaning did. He liked to cite the English phrase *cellar door* — dull in sense, lovely in pure sound — as proof that we hear music in words whether we notice or not.

> Tolkien did not write languages for his world. He grew a world in the shape of his languages.

## Grimm's Law, but make it fiction

Here is what separates Tolkien from every conlanger before him. He did not simply *write* Quenya and Sindarin — he **derived** them. He composed a proto-language, Primitive Elvish, and then applied centuries of invented but rule-bound sound changes to evolve two daughters, exactly the way our sound-change article derives English *father* and Latin *pater* from one Proto-Indo-European word. Quenya and Sindarin are cognate languages: the same ancient root surfaces in both, transformed by each one's private laws.

That is why Elvish feels old. Its irregularities are *earned* — fossils of sound changes that "happened" in fictional millennia, planted by a man who spent his working life reconstructing exactly such changes in real ones. Tolkien took historical linguistics' great trick — running sound laws backwards to recover dead languages — and ran it forwards to give life to imaginary ones.

## The name machine

You have read more Elvish than you think. Tolkien's place names are transparent compounds in his languages: *Mor-dor* "black land", *Mor-ia* "black pit", *Mith-randir* "grey wanderer" — Gandalf's Elvish name. The map of Middle-earth is a lexicon in disguise, and the names feel solid because they *are* solid: each one parses, each element recurs, the way *Cambridge* and *Oxford* parse in English. Our names-and-places article shows real maps remembering history; Tolkien built a map that remembers a history that never happened.

He never finished. The languages shifted for sixty years as his taste changed — scholars now reconstruct *phases* of Quenya as if excavating a real language's history. That, too, is the lesson of his secret vice: even with a single author and no speakers, a language refuses to hold still. Invented or not, it behaves like the living thing this whole site describes.
