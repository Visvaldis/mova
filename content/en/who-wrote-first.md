Ask a textbook who invented writing and it will say: the Sumerians, in Mesopotamia, around 3300 BCE. Clay tokens turned into clay tablets, accounting turned into literature, and the rest is history. It is a clean, satisfying story. It is also incomplete. Egypt may have gotten there at almost the same moment, possibly on its own. And if you relax the definition just a little, there are mysterious marks on pottery and turtle shells thousands of years older. The real question isn't just "who was first?" but "what are we even counting?"

## The Sumerian case

The strongest claim belongs to Sumer because Sumer left the best paper trail. Archaeologist Denise Schmandt-Besserat traced the whole evolution: small clay tokens used for millennia to count goods, then tokens sealed in clay envelopes, then impressions on the envelopes standing in for the tokens themselves. By ~3400 BCE the city of Uruk was stamping abstract signs into wet clay — the earliest cuneiform. The first documents are grain inventories, not poems.

What clinches it is the gradient: we can watch notation become writing, step by step, over centuries. No other tradition has such a complete fossil record. The rebus principle — using a sign for its sound, not its picture — appears by roughly 3100 BCE, and once signs encode sounds, anything sayable becomes writable.

## Egypt says "not so fast"

In 1988 the German archaeologist Gunter Dreyer excavated Tomb U-j at Abydos in Upper Egypt and found some 200 small bone and ivory tags, each inscribed with one or two signs. The tomb dates to Dynasty 0, around 3250 BCE — barely a century after the earliest Uruk tablets, and perhaps contemporary with them. The tags seem to record the origin of goods buried with the tomb's owner, a pre-dynastic ruler.

The uncomfortable question: did Egypt invent writing independently, or borrow the idea from Mesopotamia? The two systems look nothing alike — no shared signs, no shared structure. Many scholars now lean toward independent invention, which would mean two separate populations crossed the same threshold within a few generations. Coincidence, or inevitability?

> "Who wrote first?" turns out to depend entirely on what you mean by "writing" — and reasonable people have drawn that line in very different places.

## What about older marks?

If writing means "any systematic marks," the timeline stretches back much further. At Jiahu in central China, tortoise shells bearing repeated symbols date to roughly 6600 BCE — four thousand years before cuneiform. In southeastern Europe, Vinca-culture pottery from the 6th millennium BCE carries recurring signs that some enthusiasts have called "Old European script." The Dispilio tablet from northern Greece (~5260 BCE) shows rows of incised marks that look organized.

But most specialists draw a line. Writing, in the strict sense, encodes a specific language: it captures speech, not just meaning. You can read a Sumerian tablet aloud in Sumerian. You cannot do that with Vinca symbols — we have no idea whether they represent a language, a tally system, ownership marks, or religious symbols. They are *systematic*, but "systematic" is not the same as "linguistic." The definition you choose determines the answer you get.

## Why does it keep happening?

Perhaps the most interesting fact is that writing was invented from scratch at least four times: Mesopotamia, Egypt, China, and Mesoamerica. Each time, the same ingredients were present — agricultural surplus, growing cities, bureaucratic complexity. Writing appears to be a convergent technology: not one genius's lightning bolt but a pattern that emerges wherever societies pass a certain threshold of complexity. The Sumerians may have been first by a century, or Egypt may have beaten them, but either way — someone was going to do it.
