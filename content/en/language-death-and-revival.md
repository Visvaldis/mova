On January 21, 2008, Marie Smith Jones died in Anchorage, Alaska. She was the last person on Earth who spoke Eyak. The language had existed for thousands of years; that morning, it stopped being anyone's language at all.

This is not rare. Of the world's ~7,000 languages, linguists classify around 40% as endangered. By many estimates a language loses its last speaker every few weeks. At current rates, half of today's languages may fall silent this century.

## How a language actually dies

Languages almost never die because their speakers die. They die because of *shift*: one generation, under economic pressure, stigma, or state policy, decides — or is forced — not to pass the language to its children. The grandparents are fluent, the parents understand but answer in the dominant language, the children catch only fragments. Three generations is all it takes.

Marie Smith Jones has many predecessors in this saddest of roll calls. Dolly Pentreath, traditionally the last native speaker of Cornish, died in 1777 in the fishing village of Mousehole. Tuone Udaina, the last speaker of Dalmatian, was killed by a road explosion in 1898 — a linguist had interviewed him just the year before. Ned Maddrell, the last native speaker of Manx, died in 1974; the recordings made of him became seeds of the language's return. Tevfik Esenç, who died in 1992, took with him Ubykh and its roughly eighty consonants — one of the richest sound systems ever documented. And Boa Sr, who survived the 2004 tsunami on the Andaman Islands, died in 2010 as the last voice of Aka-Bo, her songs preserved on tape.

What's lost is not just words. Each language encodes its own classification of plants, weather, kinship, and time — knowledge accumulated over millennia and often written nowhere. As David Harrison puts it, we are losing entire libraries that were never printed.

> A language is the only museum whose entire collection fits inside a human head — and vanishes with it.

## The resurrection that worked

Now the counter-story. In 1880, Hebrew had not been anyone's mother tongue for roughly 1,700 years — it lived in prayer and scholarship, like Latin. One obsessive revivalist, Eliezer Ben-Yehuda, famously raised his son as the first native Hebrew speaker in centuries, coined thousands of modern words, and pushed Hebrew into schools. Within two generations, Hebrew went from liturgical language to the native tongue of a society — today around nine million speakers. It remains the only complete revival of a language with zero native speakers, and the existence proof for everyone who came after.

## The revival playbook

Modern revivals follow a recognizable recipe, visible in the success stories. **Welsh**: legal status, mandatory schooling, S4C television — now growing among the young. **Māori**: *kōhanga reo*, "language nests" where elders immerse preschoolers — a model copied worldwide, including Hawaiian's *Pūnana Leo*. **Cornish and Manx** show even a "dead" language with good records can return to families — Ned Maddrell's tapes did exactly that. **Wampanoag**, in Massachusetts, went further still: silent for more than a century, it was reawakened entirely from written records by Jessie Little Doe Baird, and her daughter became its first native speaker in seven generations. The common ingredients: prestige (the language must feel like the future, not the past), schools, media, state status — and above all, the hardest one, intergenerational use at home. A million apps cannot replace one grandmother.

The previous article in our Ukrainian series described the largest revival happening right now. And within Ukraine, the same drama runs at smaller scale: Crimean Tatar, classified as severely endangered after the 1944 deportation, is the object of intense revival work — corpora, schooling, broadcasting — under the hardest possible conditions.

Languages die by shift. The good news of the last half-century is that shift has a reverse gear.
