Toponyms — place names — are the most durable words in any language. Cities burn, empires rotate, whole languages die, but names soak into the landscape and stay. A map is a transcript of everyone who ever lived there.

## Kyi's city and a country called "land"

**Київ / Kyiv** carries a legend in its grammar: per the Primary Chronicle, the city of three brothers and a sister — Kyi, Shchek, Khoryv, and Lybid — is "Kyi's town," with the old possessive suffix *-ів* doing the remembering. Whatever the historical truth, the name itself is a thousand-year-old sentence fragment: *whose city?* — *Kyi's*.

**Україна** is a livelier argument. The traditional etymology reads it as "borderland" (*окраїна*) — a frame happily amplified by imperial politics. But Ukrainian linguists, notably Hryhoriy Pivtorak, argue *країна / у-країна* meant simply "land, country, one's own territory" (from *краяти*, "to cut" — a cut-out portion of land), attested in chronicles from 1187. The debate is a perfect specimen of this site's recurring theme: etymology is never politically neutral when empires are involved.

The rest of the Ukrainian map reads like the contact-layers article in stone: *Дніпро* is Iranian-Scythian water-naming; *Крим* is Turkic; dozens of *-слав* and *-город* towns are pure Slavic; *Одеса* is a Greek revival name chosen by decree.

## People are toponyms too

Personal names are portable etymology. *Володимир* is Slavic "great in power" (folk-reinterpreted as "ruler of the world"); *Богдан* is transparently "given by God"; *Oksana* is the Ukrainian form of Greek *Xenia*, "hospitality." English names tell the same layered story in another alphabet: *William* is Germanic "will-helmet," *Sarah* is Hebrew "princess." And surnames are a census of medieval jobs and jokes: English *Smith* and *Baker* match Ukrainian *Коваль* ("smith") and *Мельник* ("miller") — the same village economy, name for name. The most common surnames across Europe are simply "the guy who works metal" in twenty languages.

> Every surname is a tiny job advertisement, still running centuries after the shop closed.

## Two calendars in one year

Month names split Europe into two philosophies. English (via Latin) walks through a Roman temple: *January* belongs to two-faced Janus, *March* to Mars, *July* and *August* to Julius and Augustus Caesar — two egos installed permanently in everyone's year. The numbers even misalign: *September* through *December* mean "seventh" through "tenth," fossils of an old ten-month calendar.

Ukrainian months, by contrast, are a nature documentary: *січень* cuts (frost or timber), *лютий* is "fierce," *березень* is birch-time, *квітень* blossoms, *травень* is grass, *серпень* is the sickle of harvest, *жовтень* yellows the leaves, *листопад* is literally "leaf-fall." Czech, Belarusian, Polish and Croatian kept similar native sets; Russian adopted the Roman one. Two systems, two worldviews: one calendar commemorates power, the other watches the weather.

Read any map, any phone book, any calendar — you are reading the oldest surviving text of the place, written by everyone at once.
