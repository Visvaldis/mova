Everything else on this site describes languages nobody designed. This article is about the other kind — languages with an author, a publication date, and sometimes a copyright lawyer. There have been about a thousand documented attempts. Their failures and escapes teach as much about language as any natural tongue.

## The dream of the perfect language

The 12th-century abbess Hildegard of Bingen built *Lingua Ignota* for mystical purposes; 17th-century philosophers tried "logical" languages sorting all concepts into taxonomies; the 19th century turned to world peace. **Volapük** (1879) gathered hundreds of thousands of enthusiasts, then tore itself apart in schisms over grammar — a perfectly natural fate for any language community.

Its successor won. **Esperanto** (1887), built by Warsaw oculist L. L. Zamenhof, was deliberately easy: sixteen grammar rules, no exceptions, transparent European vocabulary. It survived two world wars (and persecution by both Hitler and Stalin), and today has up to two million learners — and, the truly strange part, perhaps a thousand *native* speakers, children of Esperantist couples. In their mouths the project stopped being a project: native kids regularize, innovate, and bend the rules — the engine from our newborn-languages article, running on synthetic fuel. An invented language, once actually spoken, immediately starts evolving like a natural one. The inventor proposes; the community disposes.

> Every successful conlang has the same biography: built as a tool, escaped as a species.

## Hollywood's linguists

The second great era of conlangs is fictional. **Klingon** (Marc Okrand, 1984) was deliberately built *alien* — object-verb-subject word order, a phonology full of rare sounds — yet it acquired translations of Shakespeare and Sunday meetups. **Na'vi**, **Dothraki**, and **High Valyrian** (David J. Peterson) raised the craft to art: full historical phonologies, designed *as if* the language had evolved — with fake sound changes obeying real Grimm-style logic. To make a believable fake language, you must simulate everything this site has described. Conlanging is linguistics' flight simulator.

## The minimalist rebellion

The newest direction inverts the old dream. Instead of a language for everything, **Toki Pona** (Sonja Lang, 2001) is a language of almost nothing: 120–140 words total. "Friend" is *jan pona* — "good person"; complexity is assembled, Lego-like, from radical simplicity. Speakers describe it as a mindfulness practice: with so few words, you must decide what things essentially *are*. It is linguistic relativity (see our language-and-thought article) turned into a usable toy — and its community, predictably, keeps coining and drifting, because no fence holds a spoken language.

## What the laboratory proves

A thousand experiments, three results. Languages can be designed, but not *controlled* — use breeds change, always. "Logical, exceptionless" grammar survives only until native children arrive. And no conlang has ever replaced a natural language, because — as the dialect-with-an-army article showed — languages win by power and identity, not by elegance of design. The conlang laboratory keeps confirming the lesson of the entire site: language is not an artifact you finish. It's a process you join.
