In 1984, Paramount had a problem: Klingons needed to talk, and "guttural noises" was no longer enough. They hired Marc Okrand, a linguist whose doctorate was on Mutsun — an extinct Native Californian language — to build a few lines of dialogue for *Star Trek III*. Okrand did something subversive. Instead of inventing impressive-sounding gibberish, he built a complete language engineered to violate human defaults.

## Designed to be alien

Every trick in Klingon is a real human feature placed where humans rarely put it. Word order: **object–verb–subject** — among the rarest arrangements in natural languages, the mirror of English. The sound system mixes consonants that genuinely exist — a retroflex *D*, a uvular *q*, the affricate *tlh* — but in a combination no human language is known to use together. The grammar is fiercely agglutinative: a Klingon verb carries chains of suffixes doing the work of entire English clauses. Nothing in Klingon is impossible; the *ensemble* is. That is what "alien" sounds like when a linguist does it: not random, but statistically perverse.

The joke is that humans immediately domesticated it. *The Klingon Dictionary* sold over three hundred thousand copies. The Klingon Language Institute, founded in 1992, publishes a journal and has produced full translations of *Hamlet* and *Much Ado About Nothing* — honoring a *Star Trek VI* joke that you haven't experienced Shakespeare until you've read him "in the original Klingon." A few dozen people speak it fluently; one linguist, d'Armond Speers, famously spoke only Klingon to his infant son. The boy, sensibly, dropped it once he noticed the language bought nothing on the playground — a tiny case study in why home transmission needs a community, as our language-death article shows. Duolingo added a Klingon course in 2018.

> Build a language alien enough and humans will prove their humanity by learning it anyway.

## The Peterson era

*Avatar* raised the bar in 2009: USC linguist Paul Frommer gave the Na'vi ejective consonants and verb infixes — and a fan community that still files vocabulary requests on his blog. Then *Game of Thrones* professionalized the field. HBO ran a contest through the Language Creation Society; David J. Peterson won it and built **Dothraki** and **High Valyrian** outward from the few dozen words in George R. R. Martin's novels.

Peterson's method is the Tolkien method industrialized: design a proto-language, apply invented sound laws, derive the on-screen language *with its history attached* — then keep evolving it season by season. High Valyrian even has its own daughter languages, decayed by fictional centuries exactly as Latin decayed into Romance. The craft has a name now — conlanging — a professional guild, and a pipeline: today a fantasy epic hires a language designer the way it hires a costume designer.

## What the fakes prove

Screen conlangs are the purest demonstration of this site's running theme. To make a language *feel* real, Okrand, Frommer, and Peterson had to simulate everything natural languages do: sound laws, irregularity, dialects, drift. Audiences can't parse Valyrian, but they can smell fakeness — a "language" that is just English with the words swapped out reads as costume, not language. Believability turns out to live exactly where linguistics said it lives: in systematic depth, not in surface exotica.

And the moment fans started actually speaking these languages, the languages started escaping — coining, drifting, regularizing beyond their creators' control, like Esperanto before them and like every tongue in this site's eighteen other stories. Paramount once went to court over who owns Klingon; the Language Creation Society's brief argued, in effect, that a spoken language belongs to its speakers. The lawyers settled. The linguistics was never in doubt.
