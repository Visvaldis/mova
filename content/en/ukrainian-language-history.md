Few European languages have a biography like Ukrainian: a millennium of continuous evolution, repeatedly banned in print, declared nonexistent by empires, and now spoken with renewed purpose by tens of millions.

## From Rus to rupture

The story begins with Old East Slavic, the language of Kyivan Rus (9th–13th centuries) — itself a granddaughter of Proto-Slavic and a great-granddaughter of Proto-Indo-European. After the Mongol destruction of Kyiv in 1240, the East Slavic dialect continuum broke apart politically, and three languages gradually crystallized: Ukrainian, Belarusian, and Russian.

The linguist George Shevelov, in his monumental *Historical Phonology of the Ukrainian Language*, argued that distinctly proto-Ukrainian sound features were forming even earlier, directly out of Proto-Slavic dialects — challenging the older model of a single unified "Old Russian" trunk. Among Ukrainian's signature developments: *o, e → i* in closed syllables (*ніч*, *кінь*), the merger of *i* and *y* into the characteristic *и*, full pleophony (*голова*, *борода* where South Slavic has *глава*, *брада*), and the vocative case, which Ukrainian kept while Russian lost it.

## The age of bans

What makes the Ukrainian story exceptional is not the linguistics — it's the politics. Researchers count dozens of imperial acts restricting the language. Two stand out. The **Valuev Circular (1863)** restricted Ukrainian-language publishing, with the notorious claim that a separate Ukrainian language "never existed, does not exist, and cannot exist." The **Ems Ukaz (1876)** went further: it banned printing and importing Ukrainian books, Ukrainian theater, and even Ukrainian lyrics under songs.

> A language does not get banned dozens of times unless it keeps working.

Literature answered. Ivan Kotliarevsky's *Eneida* (1798) — a burlesque retelling of Virgil in the living vernacular — is conventionally the start of modern literary Ukrainian. Taras Shevchenko's *Kobzar* (1840) made the peasant vernacular into a national literary language of the first rank.

## The Soviet rollercoaster

The 1920s *korenizatsiia* policy briefly promoted Ukrainian in schools and publishing — followed by a brutal reversal: the writers of the 1930s "Executed Renaissance" were repressed or killed, the 1928 orthography was scrapped as "nationalist" (the letter ґ was abolished in 1933), and decades of soft and hard russification followed, pushing Ukrainian out of cities, science, and prestige domains.

## Revival, in real time

Ukrainian became the state language in 1989, ahead of independence in 1991. The letter ґ returned in 1990. But the deepest shift is recent: after 2014, and overwhelmingly after the full-scale invasion of 2022, millions of habitual Russian speakers switched to Ukrainian by choice — one of the fastest large-scale language shifts ever observed in Europe. Ukrainian-language publishing, music, and social media exploded; surzhyk (the Ukrainian-Russian mixed speech) became an object of both study and debate rather than mere stigma.

For a linguist, Ukraine right now is a living laboratory of language revival — proof that the direction of language shift, usually toward the dominant language, can be reversed by collective will.
