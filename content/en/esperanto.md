In the 1870s, the city of Białystok — then in the Russian Empire — ran on four languages that despised each other. Poles, Russians, Germans, and Yiddish-speaking Jews shared streets and markets and almost nothing else. A schoolboy named Ludwik Lejzer Zamenhof watched the insults fly across language lines and drew a child's conclusion that became a life's work: people hate each other because they cannot understand each other. So he would build understanding — grammatically.

In 1887, by then an eye doctor, Zamenhof published a forty-page booklet under the pseudonym *Doktoro Esperanto* — "the doctor who hopes." The pseudonym swallowed the project: the language itself became Esperanto, the hoping language.

## Sixteen rules, no exceptions

Zamenhof's design bet was radical simplicity with total regularity. Every noun ends in *-o*, every adjective in *-a*, plural adds *-j*. Verbs conjugate by tense alone — *-as* now, *-is* before, *-os* after — for every verb, with no irregulars, ever. Stress always falls on the second-to-last syllable. The entire grammar fits in sixteen rules, frozen in 1905 in the *Fundamento*, the language's untouchable constitution.

The real genius, though, is the word machine. Esperanto builds vocabulary the way children build Lego. Take *san-* (health): *sana* healthy, *malsana* sick (the prefix *mal-* flips any meaning), *malsanulo* a sick person (*-ul-* = person), *malsanulejo* — "sick-person-place" — a hospital. One root, one rulebook, a whole word family. Learners report the eerie feeling of *predicting* words they have never seen — and being right.

> Esperanto is the only language where you can be fluent in words you've never heard.

## The language dictators feared

A neutral language for everyone sounds harmless. Two regimes disagreed. Hitler attacked Esperanto in *Mein Kampf* as a tool of Jewish world conspiracy; in occupied Europe its teaching was banned, and Zamenhof's three children were all murdered in the Holocaust. Stalin's terror branded Esperantists "spies of the international bourgeoisie" — in 1937–38, Soviet Esperanto activists were arrested, deported, and shot in the thousands. Ulrich Lins's history of this is bluntly titled *Dangerous Language*.

Why would dictators fear sixteen grammar rules? Because the language carried what Zamenhof called the *interna ideo* — the internal idea: that speakers of a neutral tongue meet as equals, across every border a regime draws. A language with no homeland is hard to conquer and impossible to flatter.

## The strangest success

Esperanto never became everyone's second language — English took that job by force of empire and economy, exactly as the dialect-with-an-army article predicts. But by every other measure of a living language, it lives. Estimates of speakers run from a hundred thousand to two million. The World Congress has met since 1905. Wikipedia in Esperanto holds hundreds of thousands of articles; Duolingo's course, launched in 2015, signed up over a million learners.

And then there is the strangest fact of all: roughly a thousand people speak Esperanto *natively* — children of international couples who met through the language. In their mouths the planned language stopped obeying the plan: native kids slur, coin, and regularize, drifting exactly like the newborn languages in our Nicaragua article. The constructed-languages survey on this site calls it the universal biography of a successful conlang: built as a tool, escaped as a species.

Zamenhof died in 1917, convinced he had failed. A century later, his language has outlived the empires that banned it — still small, still hoping, and still the only language in history whose native speakers exist because two strangers once agreed to hope in the same grammar.
