Here is a perfect story: elegant Victorian passengers to India booked cabins "Port Out, Starboard Home" — the shady side both ways — and the ticket stamp **P.O.S.H.** became the word *posh*. It's tidy, vivid, repeatable. It is also completely false: no such ticket has ever been found, and *posh* appears in slang before the acronym era.

Etymology attracts myths the way ruins attract treasure hunters. This article is about the fakes — and about the stranger fact that sometimes the mistake *wins*.

## The acronym plague

A reliable rule: **almost no word older than the 20th century comes from an acronym.** Acronyms barely existed before widespread literacy and bureaucracy. So *golf* is not "Gentlemen Only, Ladies Forbidden" (it's Scots, from medieval games), and the popular obscene "acronym origins" for four-letter words are all fabrications. When you see a neat acronym story about an old word, you're almost certainly looking at a backronym — an explanation invented *after* the word.

Other classics of the genre: *sirloin* was not knighted "Sir Loin" by a delighted king (it's French *sur* + *loigne*, "above the loin"); the *rule of thumb* did not come from a law about beating wives (no such law existed; the phrase is from estimation by craftsmen); and Roman soldiers, as our everyday-words article warned, were probably never literally paid in salt.

> A fake etymology is a story optimized for retelling. A real one is optimized for evidence. They evolve under different selection pressures — which is why the fakes are usually better stories.

## Ukrainian has its own folklore

Every language with a contested history grows etymological legends, and Ukrainian — see our history article — grew them under political pressure from every direction. Folk classics include deriving *козак* from *коза* ("goat" — it's actually Turkic, "free man, adventurer"), and romantic readings of *Україна* that skip the actual chronicle evidence. At the toxic end, pseudo-etymology becomes propaganda: imperial myths "proving" Ukrainian is a dialect, or pseudoscientific claims that Ukrainian (or Sanskrit-via-Ukrainian) is the world's oldest language. The method article applies here too: real etymology shows its sound correspondences and says "unclear" when stuck. Myth never says "unclear."

## When the mistake becomes the word

The respectable twin of fake etymology is **folk etymology** as a force of change: speakers misanalyze an opaque word into familiar parts, and the misanalysis sticks. English *bridegroom* should be *bridegoom* (Old English *guma*, "man") — but when *guma* died, speakers patched in *groom*. *Penthouse* was *apentis* (a lean-to), reshaped by *house*. *Cockroach* is Spanish *cucaracha* squeezed into *cock* + *roach*. *Female* isn't related to *male* at all — Latin *femella* was respelled by attraction. Ukrainian examples surface in dialect and loan adaptation, where unfamiliar foreign words get bent toward familiar roots.

This is the punchline of the whole series: the "errors" are not noise. Mishearing, reanalysis, and irresistible stories are *mechanisms* of language change — the same forces that built creoles, shifted consonants, and coined *мрія*. The difference between a myth and a word is that a word doesn't need to be true. It only needs to be repeated.
