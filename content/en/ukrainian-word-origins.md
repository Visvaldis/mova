Take a Ukrainian sentence apart and you hold pieces of wildly different ages: words older than the pyramids next to words younger than the steam engine — including one famous word that has a birthday and an author.

<!-- INTERACTIVE -->

## The deep core

The oldest layer of Ukrainian descends straight from Proto-Slavic and, behind it, Proto-Indo-European. *Мати* ("mother") is essentially the same word our steppe ancestors said six millennia ago (\*méh₂tēr). *Брат*, *сестра*, *два*, *три*, *небо*, *вода*, *жито* — this inherited core is small, maybe a couple thousand roots, but it does most of the daily work: kinship, body, numbers, weather, grain.

Some core words carry mythology inside them. *Вирій* — the warm land where birds vanish each autumn — preserves a pre-Christian Slavic otherworld; the word survived the religion that built it. *Ведмідь* is the taboo "honey-eater" who replaced the bear's forbidden true name. And *лелека* ("stork"), the most Ukrainian of birds, is actually an immigrant — borrowed from Turkic *leylek*, ultimately echoing the bird's clattering call in Arabic *laqlaq*. Even national symbols have passports.

## The contact layers

Above the core lie the borrowings, each stratum a chapter of history this site has met before: Greek words through the church (*церква* itself, *ангел*, *книга* possibly via Gothic), Turkic words from the steppe frontier (*козак*, *майдан*, *кавун*, *тютюн*), German craft and town words through Polish (*цегла* from *Ziegel*, *дах* from *Dach*, *друкувати* from *drucken*, *ринок* from *Ring*), and a modern flood of English. A Ukrainian house is practically a museum diorama: the *хата* may be ancient Iranian-contact vocabulary, its *дах* is German, the *цибуля* in the garden came through Polish from Latin *cepulla*.

> In a single phrase — козак на майдані п'є каву під дахом — four empires hand you their business cards.

## Words with birth certificates

The most surprising layer: words that were *deliberately invented*, mostly in the 19th century, when writers were building a full modern vocabulary under the bans described in our history article. The crown jewel is **мрія** ("dream, reverie") — coined by writer Mykhailo Starytsky from the verb *мріти* ("to shimmer dimly"). It filled a real gap, caught on instantly, and today feels so native that most speakers would swear it's ancient. Starytsky and his contemporaries are credited with dozens more, including *майбутнє* ("the future") and *байдужість* ("indifference").

There may be no better proof of this series' core idea — that languages are living, made things. A poet saw his language lacked a word for "dream," so he dreamed one up. An aircraft, the largest ever built, was later named after it.

## How to read a word's papers

The tool for all of this is the academic *Етимологічний словник української мови* — six volumes tracing tens of thousands of words, now searchable online. Etymology here is method, not vibes: regular sound correspondences (our Grimm's Law article shows the logic) decide between inheritance, borrowing, and coincidence. When the correspondences don't add up, the dictionary honestly says *неясно* — "unclear." Real etymology is comfortable saying "we don't know"; that's how you can tell it from the myths in our next article.
