Every word you speak is a tiny archaeological site. Dig down a few layers and ordinary vocabulary turns out to be built from frozen metaphors — pictures people stopped seeing centuries ago, but kept pronouncing.

<!-- INTERACTIVE -->

## Frozen pictures

Flex your **muscle**. Romans thought the bulge rippling under the skin looked like a small mouse running — *musculus*, "little mouse." Call something a **disaster** and you are practicing astrology: Italian *disastro*, "ill-starred," from *dis-* + *astro* — a calamity written in the heavens. Your **companion** is, literally, your with-bread person: Latin *com* + *panis*, someone you break bread with. **Sarcasm** is nastier than you thought: Greek *sarkazein*, "to tear flesh like a dog."

A **candidate** is "a person in dazzling white" — Roman office-seekers bleached their togas (*candidus*) to look trustworthy. **Salary** traces to Latin *salarium*, connected to *sal*, "salt" — though beware: the popular tale that Roman soldiers were literally *paid in salt* is a later embroidery, a perfect specimen for our myths article. And a **deadline** was once exactly that: a line around a Civil War prison camp; cross it and you'd be shot.

> Etymology is the art of noticing that people once *saw* something where we now only *say* something.

## Metaphors that run in herds

These aren't isolated curiosities — whole semantic neighborhoods share one buried image. Money words keep livestock: **pecuniary** from Latin *pecu*, "cattle"; **fee** from Old English *feoh*, also "cattle"; **capital** from *caput*, "head" — as in head of cattle. Emotion words are body-mechanics: **anxiety** from *angere*, "to choke"; **remorse** from *re-mordere*, "to bite back." Understanding words are grabbing words: **comprehend**, **grasp**, **catch** — in language after language, to understand is to seize with the hand, which may itself be a fossil of how cognition feels.

Ukrainian preserves its own gallery. *Вікно* ("window") grows from *око*, "eye" — a window is the house's eye, an image English once had too: *window* is Old Norse *vindauga*, "wind-eye." *Ведмідь* ("bear") is a taboo replacement meaning "honey-eater" — Slavs avoided the bear's true name lest they summon it, and the original Indo-European word (\*h₂ŕ̥tḱos, which gave Latin *ursus*) was lost entirely. Fear, etymologically, is the most powerful editor.

## Doublets: one word, two journeys

Sometimes the same word enters a language twice by different routes and splits in meaning. Latin *cattivus* ("captive") reached English as both **captive** (straight from Latin) and **caitiff**; *regalis* arrived as **royal** (through French) and **regal** (from Latin directly). **Skirt** and **shirt** are the same Germanic garment — one stayed home, one went viking and came back Norse. Spotting doublets is like meeting twins separated at birth: same DNA, different accents.

None of this knowledge is necessary to speak. That's what makes it delightful: beneath the most boring sentence — *the candidate's salary was a disaster* — runs a hidden film strip of white togas, salt roads, and unlucky stars. Once you learn to x-ray words, no sentence is boring again.
