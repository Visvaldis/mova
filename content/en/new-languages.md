Languages usually change slowly, over centuries. But occasionally a new language is born within a single human lifetime — and the births follow a startling pattern: the inventors are almost always children.

<!-- INTERACTIVE -->

## The Nicaragua experiment nobody planned

Before 1977, deaf children in Nicaragua grew up isolated, each with only the simple "home signs" used with their families. Then the country opened its first schools for the deaf in Managua. The teaching method (lip-reading, Spanish) failed completely. The playground succeeded beyond anyone's imagination.

Thrown together, hundreds of children pooled their home signs into a rough common system. Then came the second cohort — younger kids who arrived when this pidgin-like system already existed. They didn't just learn it; they *transformed* it. Signing became faster, more fluid, and — crucially — grammatical: consistent word order, verb agreement, spatial grammar to track who did what to whom. Linguist Ann Senghas showed the pattern cohort by cohort: where older signers expressed "rolling down" as one holistic gesture, younger ones broke it into discrete parts — *roll* + *down* — recombinable like words. That's the combinatorial signature of true language.

> No one taught them grammar. The grammar came from the children.

Nicaraguan Sign Language (ISN) is now a full language with native signers, poetry, and ongoing change — and the only time in history scientists watched a language emerge from nothing, in real time.

## Pidgins and creoles: the older story

The same drama played out, more slowly and brutally, around plantations and ports for centuries. Adults thrown together without a common language build a **pidgin** — a stripped-down, no-frills code: small vocabulary, little grammar, nobody's mother tongue. But when children grow up hearing pidgin as their primary input, they do what the second Nicaraguan cohort did: they expand it into a **creole** — a complete natural language with stable grammar. Haitian Creole, Tok Pisin in Papua New Guinea, and Jamaican Patois all carry this origin story.

Linguist Derek Bickerton argued creoles look grammatically similar worldwide because children fall back on an innate "bioprogram" — a default grammar wired into the human brain. The strong version is fiercely debated (creolists point to continuity from the parent languages instead), but the core observation stands: give children fragmentary input, and they return a full system.

## Why this matters for the whole field

These newborn languages are the closest thing we have to replaying the origin of language itself. They suggest that the capacity explored in our first article isn't a fragile cultural inheritance — it's a force, present in every child, waiting for a community to ignite it. Language, it turns out, is not something children merely receive. It is something they *build* — and they only need each other to do it.
